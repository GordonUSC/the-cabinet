A vocabulary for motion

Ones, twos,
and the seam.

Five terms, each with a plate, an example, a counterexample and a place to look.

Craft term, verified

On ones, on twos

Animating on ones draws a new image for every frame, 24 per second. On twos, each drawing is held for two frames, 12 per second.

Twos read as weight, hesitation or hand-made texture. Ones read as fluency and speed. A film can switch per character and per moment.

Example: In the forest swing, Miles is drawn on twos to show his inexperience while Peter B. Parker is on ones for smoother movement.

Not this: A single fixed rate for every character would erase the contrast that makes the choice legible.

Where to look: The forest swing clip, 0:00 to 2:09, where both swing together. The first nine minutes never show them side by side.

Craft term, verified

Smear frame

A deliberately distorted in-between drawing that carries fast motion across a cut instead of a camera-style motion blur.

Stretched limbs, multiple hands, a blurred shape drawn by hand rather than computed. Older 2D technique revived inside a CG film.

Example: Into the Spider-Verse was animated without motion blur, using smears instead.

Not this: Rendered motion blur would smooth the same action into photographic realism.

Where to look: Any fast gesture in the opening; pause and step a frame.

Course glossary, Session 11

Calling card

A distinctive visual element, move or style that identifies a creator or character.

Ben-Day dots and deliberately misaligned color, as if the print registration slipped. Panels and lettering inside the frame.

Example: The trailer: nobody else's film looks like this.

Not this: House style shared by a studio is not a calling card; a calling card belongs to one voice.

Where to look: Trailer, 0:00 to 2:40.

Course glossary, Session 11

Chimera

An image or form composed of multiple disparate elements; a hybrid or composite visual approach.

Several animation languages sharing one frame without resolving into one.

Example: The opening, 0:00 to 9:00: comic ink, CG light, hand-drawn effects in one shot.

Not this: A consistent single style, however rich, is not a chimera.

Where to look: Anywhere the effects animation is visibly drawn over the CG.

Second text, verified production facts

2D over 3D, seam visible

Hand-drawn characters composited over computer-animated destruction, with the difference left legible rather than hidden.

Production I.G with Studio Khara on kaiju design; lead 3D animation director Masaru Matsumoto with a small team on 3ds Max and Blender; the directors chose which sequences would be 3D at storyboard.

Example: Kaiju No. 8, episode 1, played in full at 10:48.

Not this: A fully rendered CG monster film hides the seam; that costs more and says less.

Where to look: Every wide shot with a kaiju in it.
