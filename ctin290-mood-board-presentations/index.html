Version 1Walk us through the board

Start with the feeling you want, show the visual choices, then explain what you would test. Maya introduces the world and palette, Devin connects the references to play, and Priya explains the curation.

Five-minute rehearsal plan. The time ranges are cue targets. Practice aloud and leave room to point, change images and hand over.

0:00–0:45

Maya

cue target

ShowShow the handoff reference. Give the class a moment to look at the hands before you speak.

Hi, we're Maya, Devin and Priya, and this is The Last Sitting. Our brief was a game involving collaboration. We started with this image of someone passing a plate across a kitchen.

What we liked is that neither person can finish the action alone. Somebody has to be ready on the other side. Our idea is a co-op game about getting through dinner service together. We want it to feel a little stressful, but also like these people know each other really well.

0:45–1:35

Maya

cue target

ShowShow the palette and two kitchen references. Point to the warm light and cooler metal.

I worked on the palette. We kept coming back to these warm, dim kitchens because they feel lived in. The cooler metal gives us some contrast around the work surfaces. That's where warm and cool could help us direct your eye.

We're still figuring out how dark we can go. We like the atmosphere, but you also need to see what you're doing. We'd want to check that with the UI on screen, especially when a few orders are happening at once. Devin worked on those screen ideas.

1:35–2:30

Devin

cue target

ShowShow the overhead layout, then the order-ticket mockup. Trace the route between two stations.

These are visual mockups for how the game could work. In this layout, the stations are far enough apart that you'd have to pass things to someone else. The blocking helps explain the collaboration: where the characters stand changes what they can do together.

For the HUD, we put the orders across the top because we want you to notice what's waiting. That's our visual hierarchy. We still need to find out whether the tickets pull too much attention away from your partner. A quick prototype would help us check that.

2:30–3:20

Devin

cue target

ShowShow the split-screen reference. Follow the same plate across the two views.

The split-screen idea is the part I'm most interested in. We'd like both players to recognize the plate as it moves between their views. If it seems to disappear and turn into a different object, that handoff could get confusing. Keeping that continuity clear matters to the mechanic.

We haven't tested this yet. We'd start with simple shapes and see whether two people can follow the plate without us explaining where to look. Then we can come back to the board and change anything that's getting in the way.

3:20–4:15

Priya

cue target

ShowShow a shared-work reference beside a quieter painting from the board. Let viewers compare them.

I worked on choosing the references and writing why they belong. The older paintings helped us think about people sharing a space, especially the quieter moments around food. We want that sense of familiarity in the characters, even when the kitchen gets busy.

We wouldn't copy every part of a reference. For one image we might want the arrangement of the bodies; for another, the lighting. Labeling that made it easier for us to tell whether we actually agreed. Sometimes we liked the same image for completely different reasons.

4:15–5:00

Priya

cue target

ShowReturn to the full board. Point to one reference that needs reconsidering, then to the opening handoff.

The character-select reference is something we'd revisit. We like how it separates the characters, but its drawing style doesn't quite match the rest of the board. We need to decide what we're borrowing from it and whether there's a clearer reference.

Our next step would be that handoff test, then a pass on the palette and UI together. For feedback, we'd love to know: looking at this board, does the collaboration come through? Which image helps you understand it, and which one feels out of place?

Version 2Start with the questions you are working through

Organize the same board around three questions: what collaboration feels like, why these references belong together, and whether the palette will help players read the game. Each teammate takes one question.

Five-minute rehearsal plan. The time ranges are cue targets. Practice aloud and leave room to point, change images and hand over.

0:00–0:45

Devin

cue target

ShowShow the full board, then the handoff reference. Keep the opening conversational.

We're presenting The Last Sitting, our co-op kitchen concept. We kept coming back to three questions while putting this board together. What do the players actually need each other for? Why do these references fit? And can you read the game when everything gets busy?

I'll start with the first one. If this reminds you of Overcooked, yeah, that's a useful comparison to discuss. The moment we're interested in is really small: you've finished your part, and now you're waiting for your teammate to be ready.

0:45–1:40

Devin

cue target

ShowShow the layout and split-screen mockup. Point to the pass between stations.

Here's where that idea shows up visually. The stations have different jobs, and the pass sits between them. We want the blocking to make that relationship easy to understand before anyone reads instructions.

In the split-screen mockup, we'd keep the plate recognizable across both views. The question is whether that helps you pay attention to your teammate or just makes you watch the edge of the screen. We don't know yet. That's something we'd test with a rough prototype. Priya worked on the references for the people using this space.

1:40–2:30

Priya

cue target

ShowShow two references with different sources. Point to the particular detail each contributes.

Our second question was why these images belong together. We have kitchen references alongside older paintings, so the connection needs to be more specific than 'we liked them.'

In the paintings, we're looking at how people share a small space and how much you can tell from their posture. In the kitchen references, we're looking at the work surfaces and light. The tone we're going for is familiar and a little worn in. Those are different pieces of the same world, and we need to make that connection visible on the board.

2:30–3:20

Priya

cue target

ShowStay on the board. Indicate a reference whose intended use needs a clearer label.

One thing we'd explain better is what each reference is for. An image can be useful for a pose without setting the style for the whole game. Right now, the drawn character reference could make that confusing, so we'd either label the specific thing we're taking from it or replace it.

We'd also keep the source credits with the images. Our contribution is how we select and connect the references, and what that helps us decide. Maya has the palette question, which is where a lot of those choices meet.

3:20–4:15

Maya

cue target

ShowShow the palette beside the HUD mockup. Point to the order indicator as well as the background.

My question was whether the palette would still work once you had to play the game. We like the warm browns because they make the kitchen feel familiar. But if everything has a similar value, an important object could disappear into the background.

The cooler steel could help separate the work surfaces. A brighter order indicator could help with visual hierarchy, too. We'd want to check its shape and contrast as well as its color, so players have more than one clue. Looking good as a board doesn't tell us whether that reads during play.

4:15–5:00

Maya

cue target

ShowReturn to the full board and leave it visible for feedback.

So those are the three questions we'd take into the next version: can you follow the handoff, do the references feel like the same world, and can you find what matters on screen?

Devin would test the handoff, Priya would tighten the references and labels, and I'd check the palette with the UI. We'd bring those results back to the board together. For feedback today, what's the first thing your eye goes to? We're curious whether that's the same thing we're trying to emphasize.

Try it with your team

Show, then explain. Give the room a moment to see the reference before you tell them what you took from it.

Use terms where they help. “Visual hierarchy” is useful when you can point to what your viewer should notice first. Explain the choice in your own words.

Keep intention and evidence separate. “We want this to feel tense” is a design intention. “Our playtesters felt tense” needs an actual playtest.

Make the handoffs easy. Agree who covers each choice and how the next person comes in. Everyone should have something meaningful to explain.

Rehearse to five minutes. Time the presentation with the images, pauses and handoffs included. Use the assignment rubric to check your board and presentation.
